# Copy tells

These are the things current models write that people rarely do. The
script catches the ones with a rule name in brackets, and most of the old
vocabulary (leverage, seamless, delve). The rest need a reader.

Read each line as a stranger would. Would a person say it out loud? Could a
competitor use it unchanged? Does the reader learn anything from it?

The first part is the fifteen tells that do the most damage, with rewrites.
The full list after it gives every other tell one line, so a cold read can
check everything.

## Sentences

**1. "It's not X, it's Y"** [negation-pivot, negation-tail]. This is the
strongest tell, because nobody suggested X in the first place.
Draft: "It's not a website builder. It's a growth engine." <!-- tropes-allow -->
Rewrite: "We build your site and keep it up to date."
The same family: "not just X but Y", "Not because X. Because Y.", "Y rather than X".
A "not" the reader needs stays: "Cash only, not cards."

**2. The comma afterthought** [with-rider, ing-rider]. A second idea gets
attached after a comma, often with "with" or an "-ing" word.
Draft: "Your site loads fast, with SEO and design done for you." <!-- tropes-allow -->
Draft: "We service every boiler we fit, ensuring peace of mind." <!-- tropes-allow -->
Rewrite: "Your site loads fast. We handle the SEO and the design."
Give each idea its own sentence and its own subject, or cut the extra one.
The same goes for a tail like "all season" or "for salons and barbershops"
that reads as tacked on.

**3. Two fragments as a heading** [joined-heading].
Draft: "Fall cleanup in Larkfield, from $90" <!-- tropes-allow -->
Rewrite: "Fall cleanup in Larkfield starts at $90"
A heading is one plain sentence or a plain label.

**4. Figures of speech** [model-tell]. For example:
"The report lands every Monday", "it hands you a brief", "trust compounds", "at its core", "this is where X comes in".
The staged reveal is the same tell: "Here's the kicker:", "Here's the thing", "And honestly?", "That changes everything", "That's the whole point."
Rewrite: "The report arrives every Monday." Use the literal verb (arrives,
shows, gives, starts), or say the point.

**5. Nobody doing anything** [order-result]. A vague verb, or work that seems
to happen by itself.
Draft: "Send us a price list and the site uses it." <!-- tropes-allow -->
Draft: "Routine maintenance runs on a schedule." <!-- tropes-allow -->
Rewrite: "Send us your price list and we update the prices page." "We
service the boiler every October."

**6. Threes everywhere** [triads]. "Fast. Simple. Reliable." Two lists of
three in one hero. Every card shaped "Builds…, tracks…, and writes…".
Use the real number of things. One list of three per section at most.

**7. Fit with no facts** [vague-fit]. For example:
"Built around how you work", "tailored to you", "your way".
Rewrite: name what changes. "Your stages, your fields, your price list."

**8. Reassurance nobody asked for.** "Real people answer the phone", "a site
that actually works", "…and nothing is lost", "…whenever you ask", "until you approve it", "so you can focus on what matters", "while you sleep".
Cut it. Say a limit or a permission once, where the reader would worry.

**9. The no-effort promise** [effort-once]. "Set it once and forget it."
Rewrite: name the step. "Book the first visit and we schedule the rest."

**10. Slogans and stock openers** [rhetorical-question]. "Your kitchen, your
rules." "Peace of mind, one visit at a time." "Tired of X? Look no
further." "In today's fast-paced world…" "When it comes to…" "Welcome to…" "Are you looking for…" "Whether you're X or Y…"
Say the offer plainly. A real question with a specific answer is fine:
"Radiators cold at the top? That's usually trapped air."

**11. Names for nothing.** "In one place", "one dashboard for everything", "the tools you already use", "however you invoice today",
"solutions", "your partner in growth".
Rewrite: name the thing. "Stripe and QuickBooks." "See every client's
bookings and bills."

**12. Same length, same shape** (by ear). Every sentence about as long as
the last. Every paragraph goes claim, example, restatement. Every lede starts "We…". Every page ends "Ready to get started?"
Mix a four-word sentence with a long one, and vary who acts. Each page's
last heading says something only that page can.

## Claims

**13. Invented proof.** Stats, studies, review counts, star ratings,
customer quotes, "trusted by thousands". These come only from the owner's
notes, with the count, the source and the date. With no source, it becomes
a `NEED:` line for the owner. Never soften it into a vaguer claim.

**14. Puffery and table stakes.** "Quality", "trusted", "professional", "the
best", "#1", or "easy online booking" and "friendly staff" offered as features.
Put the evidence beside it (the licence number, the review count and where,
the years) or cut it.

**15. Ads about the reader's condition** [personal-attribute]. "Struggling
with debt?" Meta rejects these. Talk about the offer instead.

## Headings, buttons and layout

- A count is not a heading. "Six apps to start from" and "Three ways we help" make the reader wait for the point. Say what the things do: "Start from a website, CRM or Work Board that already works". A number with no list beside it ("15 towns") gets its list.
- A button names what the reader gets [weak-cta]: "Book a survey" over
  "Learn more". "Get started" is fine for a product people sign up to.
- A heading stays under 12 words [long-heading]. The line under it adds
  the fact the heading couldn't hold [restates-heading].
- One phrase in two sections is repetition [repeated-phrase], except the
  call to action, which is worded the same everywhere.
- "You" at least as often as "we" [we-over-you]. An about section may say
  "we".
- No em dashes [em-dash]. Use a comma, a colon or a new sentence.
- Sentence case, not title case. Bold only what a skimmer must see, no emoji
  bullets, and no `**` or `#` showing on the page.

## The full list

Everything the fifteen above don't cover, one line per tell: the tell, an
example, then the fix.

### Words

- **Verbs dressed up:** "leverage", "harness", "unlock", "empower", "elevate", "streamline", "navigate". Use the plain verb for the action: use, open, cut, book.
- **Inflated adjectives:** "seamless", "robust", "world-class", "innovative", "comprehensive". Give a number, a material or a limit instead.
- **Inflated importance:** "a testament to", "plays a pivotal role", "underscores the importance of", "paves the way". Say what happened and to whom.
- **Brochure words:** "nestled", "in the heart of", "boasts", "vibrant", "rich heritage". Say where it is and what it has.
- **Metaphor nouns:** "tapestry", "realm", "landscape", "ecosystem", "journey", "solutions". Name what the business sells.
- **Today's model verbs used as figures:** "carries two costs", "the risk lives in", "it all turns on trust", "names the problem", "the shape of it", "hold both at once". Use the literal verb.
- **Model filler words:** "honestly", "genuinely", "quietly", "real" ("the real problem"), "the right question", "most people". Cut them.
- **Consultant and finance talk:** "north star", "key takeaways", "the forward case", "best-in-class operators". Say it in the owner's words.
- **Era words in bulk:** "delve", "intricate", "meticulous", "showcasing", "fostering". One is fine. Several in a page is a tell.
- **Boosters:** "significantly", "remarkably", "incredibly", "truly". Give the figure, or cut it.
- **Small-business clichés:** "we're passionate about", "we pride ourselves", "your partner in", "making X simple". Say a fact only this business could say.
- **Saying "serves as" for "is":** "serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "offers", "features". Write "is" or "has".

### Sentences

- **Staccato runs:** "No fluff. No filler. Just results." Write one plain sentence with the real claim.
- **"It also…" to finish a lede.** Make it its own point or cut it.
- **Ownership and limits as a refrain:** "your own", "its own", "only", "never" in every section. Say a permission once, as what a person can do.
- **Circular lines:** "solutions that solve your problems", "results that matter". Say what it produces.
- **Fake balance:** "While X has merits, Y offers benefits." Pick one and say why.
- **Hedges:** "it's worth noting", "it's important to note", "it goes without saying". Cut them. If something is uncertain, say what.
- **Flat punctuation:** long sentences joined with "and", hardly a comma, never an aside. Break at the "and", and let one sentence carry an aside.

### Openings and closings

- **Announcing the structure:** "I'm going to make three points", "Let's break it down", "Let's explore". Just make the points.
- **Restating the question before answering it.** Answer it.
- **Summary closers:** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "In summary", "Remember, …". Stop at the last fact or the call to action.
- **A one-liner close that restates the thesis**, or one that gestures at stakes ("the reckoning will come"). End on the fact or the action.

### Headings and layout

- **Formula headings:** "X, reimagined", "Where X meets Y", "Everything you need, all in one place". Write a heading a stranger could repeat.
- **Echoes:** a heading that repeats the company tagline, a customer quote used as a heading, a closing heading that repeats its button. Rewrite from the reader's question.
- **The same pattern everywhere:** every heading in the same shape, every card opening "Builds… / Tracks… / Writes…", the same phrase ("…in minutes") in headings across a site. Mix labels and claims, and vary who starts the line.
- **Process in the hero:** call, quote, schedule as the first thing. Lead with what the reader gets.
- **Formatting tells:** bold stems on every bullet, a "Bold term: explanation" list, horizontal rules between sections, a heading every 80 words. Plain paragraphs, and fewer headings.
- **Broetry:** one line a paragraph, building to a moral. Two or three sentences a paragraph, varied.

### Articles and longer prose

- **Discovery arcs:** "I didn't plan it, but", "the thing I keep coming back to", "here's where I landed", "what got me was". Say the finding.
- **Judging what's interesting:** "the most interesting part", "my favourite detail". Show the detail and let the reader judge.
- **Crowned superlatives:** "the best thing you can do is", "the one that surprised me most". Say the thing.
- **False-modest asides:** "I want to be careful here", "to be fair". Cut them.
- **Stock figures:** "at the end of the day", "we've seen this movie before", "hit a nerve", "doing the heavy lifting", "load-bearing". Say it literally.
- **Invented biography:** "I've run kitchens for twenty years" with no source. Only the owner's real history, from the notes.
- **Placeholders left in:** "[INSERT LINK]", "2025-XX-XX". Find the fact or ask with a `NEED:` line.

### Claims

- **Vague attribution:** "studies show", "experts agree", "industry reports". Name the source, or cut it.
- **Generic examples:** a case study any business could run. Use one real job, place, number or name per section.
- **Fake urgency:** "only 3 left", a countdown. Only when it's true and sourced.
